Pour over brewing sits at an intersection that other methods don't quite reach: enough manual control to actually improve your results over time, simple enough equipment that nothing mechanical can fail you. The [Hario V60](/coffee-makers/) is the format most serious home brewers reach for first, and for good reason , the design hasn't changed much because it doesn't need to.

The problem is that "Hario V60" covers more ground than most buyers expect. There's the bare dripper, the starter set, the complete bundle, and a couple of non-Hario alternatives worth considering depending on what's already in your kitchen. What separates a good purchase from a frustrating one is understanding which version of this setup actually matches your situation.

## What to Look For in a Pour Over Coffee Maker

### Material and Heat Retention

The V60 cone comes in ceramic, plastic, glass, and metal. Ceramic is the traditional choice, and there are real reasons it holds up as the recommendation for most buyers. It retains heat well once prewarmed, doesn't contribute off-flavors, and the thermal mass is consistent enough that your brew temperature doesn't swing between pours. Plastic V60s are lighter and more forgiving for travel, but they're a different product for a different use case.

Heat retention matters more than it sounds. If the dripper is cold when you start, the first pour drops significantly in temperature before it reaches the coffee bed. That affects extraction. Prewarming with hot water before brewing is the fix, and it works , but ceramic rewards this habit where thin-walled plastic barely responds to it.

### Dripper Size

Size 01 brews one cup. Size 02 brews one to four cups, which in practice means one to two cups at the volumes most people actually pull. For home use, the 02 is the right call almost every time. If you're brewing for one and the 01 appeals as a tidy single-serve setup, the trade-off is reduced margin for error , the smaller cone punishes uneven pours more noticeably.

That's not coincidence. The 02 is more versatile and more forgiving, which matters when you're still developing technique.

### What Comes in the Box

A bare dripper is the most flexible option if you already own a gooseneck kettle, a scale, a server, and filters. If you don't own those pieces, a set or bundle closes the gap faster and usually more economically than buying components separately.

The meaningful items to look for in any set are: a server or carafe to brew into (glass is fine, it just requires care), paper filters matched to the cone size, and a lid or cover for the server if you're not drinking immediately. A measuring scoop is marginally useful; a scale is far more useful and typically sold separately regardless. Browse the full range of [manual brewing options](/coffee-makers/) before deciding whether a bundle or bare dripper fits your starting point.

### Paper Filters vs. Metal Filters

Hario's tabbed paper filters are the standard choice for V60 brewing, and for most people they should be. Paper filters capture fine particles and oils, producing a cleaner cup with more clarity in the flavor.

Neither is objectively better. If you want clean, bright, and fruit-forward from a light roast, paper is the right tool. If you prefer a fuller, richer cup and don't want to keep buying consumables, metal is a legitimate preference. The choice also affects grind size and flow rate, so switching filter types mid-setup usually means recalibrating your pour technique.

### Grinder and Kettle Matters More Than the Dripper

This applies to pour over more than almost any other brewing method. The cone itself , ceramic, glass, or otherwise , is not where the outcome is determined. Grind consistency and water temperature control are. A cheap blade grinder produces uneven particle sizes that extract unevenly regardless of how carefully you pour. An inconsistent water temperature produces a flat or sour cup that no amount of pour technique can rescue.

If budget is the constraint, spend it on a burr grinder before upgrading the dripper. A mid-range hand grinder and a budget V60 will beat an expensive dripper paired with a blade grinder every time. The same logic applies in espresso , and it applies here too.

## Buying Guide

### Which Version of the V60 Setup Is Actually Right for You

The most common mistake buyers make with pour over equipment is treating the dripper as the primary purchase decision when it's actually the secondary one. Before choosing between a bare cone, a starter set, or a full bundle, the more useful question is: what do you already own?

If you have a gooseneck kettle and a burr grinder, the bare V60 dripper or the ceramic set is the cleaner choice. If you own neither, start with the full bundle and allocate the remaining budget toward a grinder. The dripper model you choose matters far less than whether you have consistent grind size and temperature control.

### Ceramic vs. Other Materials

Ceramic is the recommendation for home use. It holds heat consistently after prewarming, it's neutral in flavor, and it lasts indefinitely with basic care. Plastic V60s have their place , they're unbreakable for travel, and Hario's clear plastic version is a legitimate choice for those use cases , but for a stationary home setup, ceramic is the more durable and thermally stable option.

Metal V60s exist and perform well, but they're typically priced at the premium end and the performance gap over ceramic for home brewing is marginal. Glass V60s are beautiful and show the brew clearly, which is useful for learning technique, but they're fragile in a way ceramic isn't.

### Paper Filters: Which Ones and Why They Matter

Hario's 02W white filters are the standard choice and the correct one for most brewers. Brown (unbleached) filters are also available and perform identically once rinsed , some brewers notice a papery taste from unrinsed brown filters, which is resolved with a 30-second hot water rinse before brewing.

Third-party filters exist and some perform well, but consistency varies. Using Hario's own filters with a Hario cone removes one variable from a process that has enough variables already. The 100-count packages are more economical than smaller packs and worth buying in that quantity once you've confirmed the brew profile you're chasing.

### The Grinder Question

The grinder is where this category of brewing is most commonly undermined. A burr grinder , hand or electric , is not optional equipment for V60 brewing; it's the foundation of consistent extraction. Blade grinders produce uneven particle sizes that result in simultaneous over- and under-extraction in the same cup. The resulting brew tastes simultaneously bitter and flat, and no pour technique compensates for it.

If the total equipment budget is limited, the right call is to spend more on the grinder and less on the dripper , not the reverse. For a broader look at how grinder choice interacts with different [coffee makers](/coffee-makers/), the category page covers the tradeoffs across brewing methods.

### Serving Volume and Daily Brewing Habits

If you're reliably brewing for three or four people, a larger format brewer or multiple sequential brews is worth considering , the V60 02 is not designed for batch brewing at scale, and pushing it past two cups produces inconsistent extraction toward the end of the pour as the cone floods.

For single-cup daily brewing, the 02 is well-matched. It's also the format with the widest filter availability and the broadest recipe library, which matters when you're using published recipes from specialty roasters to dial in your technique.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between the Hario V60 bare dripper and a starter set?

The bare [HARIO V60 Dripper 02 White](product:hario-v60-dripper-02-white) is the cone only , you supply the server, filters, and kettle separately. A starter set includes those components packaged together by Hario, which means everything is size-matched and compatible out of the box. If you're starting from zero equipment, the starter set is the more practical entry point. If you already own pour over accessories, the bare dripper avoids redundancy.
### Do I need a gooseneck kettle to brew V60 coffee?

A gooseneck kettle is not strictly required, but it produces meaningfully better results. The narrow spout gives you precise control over flow rate and pour placement, both of which affect how evenly the coffee bed saturates and extracts. A standard kettle can work for initial experimentation, but uneven saturation , which is harder to avoid without a gooseneck , is one of the most common causes of flat or sour pour over results.
### Is the Bodum pour over comparable to the Hario V60?

They are both manual pour over brewers, but they produce different cups. The Bodum uses a reusable stainless steel mesh filter, which allows coffee oils and fine particles to pass through and produces a fuller-bodied, heavier result. The V60 with paper filters produces a cleaner, brighter cup with more flavor clarity. Neither is better in absolute terms , the right choice depends on whether you prefer body and richness or clarity and brightness in your cup.
### How many cups can the V60 size 02 actually brew at once?

Hario rates the 02 for one to four cups, but practical home brewing at the 40, 60g coffee range typically produces one to two substantial mugs. Pushing to four cups requires a longer, more complex pour sequence and produces less consistent extraction as the cone floods near the end. For single-cup or two-cup daily brewing, the 02 is well-suited. For regularly brewing three or four cups, a larger format brewer handles the volume more reliably.
### What grind size should I use for V60 brewing?

Medium-fine is the standard starting point , coarser than espresso, finer than French press, roughly the texture of coarse table salt. Adjust based on brew time: if your total brew finishes well under three minutes, grind finer; if it drags past four minutes, grind coarser. Grind size matters more than almost any other variable, which is why a burr grinder is the most important piece of equipment in the V60 setup.
