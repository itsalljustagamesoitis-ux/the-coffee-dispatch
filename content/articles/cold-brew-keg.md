Most "cold brew keg" searches land on actual kegging equipment , CO2 systems, cornelius kegs, draft towers. What most people searching that phrase actually want is a dedicated vessel that brews a batch, lives in the fridge, and dispenses cleanly without a mess. A pitcher or jar built for the job handles that better than a full keg setup for most home kitchens.

Making your own cold brew from quality beans , something like a coarse grind from Heart or Coava , costs a fraction of bottled options. Stumptown's ready-to-drink cold brew is excellent, but at that per-bottle price you're paying several times what homemade costs. For daily consumption, the case for making it yourself is obvious. The [Cold Brew & Iced Coffee](/cold-brew/) category is where that argument actually lands.

## What to Look For in a Cold Brew Brewing Vessel

### Capacity and How You Actually Drink Cold Brew

Cold brew is a batch process. You're not pulling a shot in 30 seconds , you're steeping coarse grounds for 12 to 24 hours, then straining and storing concentrate or ready-to-drink coffee for the next several days. Capacity matters more here than with most brewing equipment because the effort involved is the same whether you're making one cup or eight.

A 2-quart vessel is the practical sweet spot for one- or two-person households. It produces enough cold brew to last four or five days without taking up unreasonable fridge real estate. If you're making cold brew for an office or regularly hosting, you need to think about commercial-scale options , or accept that you're running the same vessel twice a week.

The ratio question is related: cold brew concentrate recipes typically run 1:4 to 1:8 coffee to water. If a vessel's labeled capacity is 64 oz, account for grounds displacement. What looks like a generous container often yields less finished liquid than expected.

### Material: Glass Versus Stainless

Glass is honest. You can see the brew progress, check the color, and know without opening the lid whether steeping is complete. It cleans thoroughly and doesn't retain odors , after a few months of daily use, a glass vessel still smells like nothing when empty. The tradeoff is fragility. In a fridge with other items, glass requires some deliberate arrangement.

Stainless steel removes the fragility concern and typically offers better insulation for transport or serving situations. The downside is opacity , you're working by schedule rather than visual cues, which matters more for beginners calibrating their first few batches. For someone who's dialled in a recipe, that's less relevant.

### Filtration and Sediment Control

How grounds are separated from the finished brew matters for both workflow and cup quality. Some vessels include a built-in mesh filter that sits inside the container during steeping and lifts out cleanly. Others rely on a separate filter or the pour-over method of straining through a fine mesh or paper. Built-in filters are more convenient but vary significantly in mesh fineness.

Sediment in finished cold brew isn't harmful but it's unpleasant. Fine grounds that slip through a coarse filter settle at the bottom and get disturbed every time the container moves. If you're using a mason jar approach, a secondary filtration step , paper filter or cloth , usually produces a cleaner result than mesh alone. This is one area where purpose-built cold brew vessels have a genuine advantage over general-use pitchers.

### Sealing and Storage

Cold brew stored correctly keeps for up to two weeks in the fridge. The container seal makes a significant difference. An airtight lid protects against oxidation and prevents the brew from absorbing ambient fridge odors , both onion-adjacent smells and the general background of a well-used refrigerator. A lid that only loosely fits or requires precise positioning to seal introduces a variable you don't want.

Pouring ergonomics belong here too. A vessel you use daily should pour without dripping or requiring a two-handed grip every time. Wide-mouth designs are easier to fill and clean but sometimes awkward to pour from without a dedicated spout. Narrower spout lids solve that at the cost of more difficult cleaning access. Exploring the full range of [cold brew equipment](/cold-brew/) options before committing to a format is genuinely worth the time , the differences between vessel types compound over daily use.

## Buying Guide

### Batch Size Matched to Drinking Frequency

The single most important purchase variable is how much cold brew you actually consume each week. A 2-quart vessel produces roughly eight 8-oz servings. For one daily drinker, that's a week of cold brew from a single batch. For two daily drinkers, it's three to four days. Neither calculation is wrong , they just determine how often you're spending 15 minutes on prep.

Over-sizing creates a different problem: cold brew keeps well, but two weeks is a realistic upper limit on quality even with proper sealing. A vessel that holds more than you can drink before quality degrades means you're either pouring coffee out or rushing to finish it.

### Integrated Filtration Versus Separate Straining

Purpose-built cold brew vessels typically include a mesh basket or filter tube that holds grounds during steeping and removes cleanly for straining. This is convenient, but convenience and quality aren't always the same thing. Coarse mesh filters pass more fine particles than paper or cloth, producing cold brew with more sediment , not a problem for some drinkers, a consistent irritant for others.

Separate filtration through paper gives you the cleanest possible cup and lets you use any airtight glass pitcher as your storage vessel. The extra step is real but the variable it removes , sediment in finished brew , is worth it if cup clarity matters to you. Browsing [cold brew brewing methods](/cold-brew/) can help calibrate what level of filtration is appropriate for your recipe and brewing time.

### Lid Design and Daily Ergonomics

A vessel you use every day will reveal lid design quality faster than any review. Pour spout lids reduce drip. Wide-mouth lids are easier to clean but require more pouring care. Airtight seals matter for flavor over days and weeks , a lid that requires precise alignment to seal properly becomes a daily frustration.

Test the lid concept before committing. A mason jar with a pour spout insert and rubber seal handles different than a vessel with an integrated hinged spout. Both work; one may fit your grip and pour angle better than the other.

### Glass Fragility as a Real Variable

Glass cold brew vessels are almost universally better than plastic equivalents for flavor neutrality and visual clarity , but fragility is a genuine cost that varies by context. In a stable fridge where the vessel lives in one spot and moves only to the counter and back, fragility is a minor concern. In a shared kitchen, a crowded fridge, or a setting where the container gets handed off between people, the risk compounds.

Stainless alternatives exist for these contexts. They sacrifice visibility but gain durability and portability.

### Scale: Home Versus High-Volume

Most readers here are solving a home kitchen problem , a vessel that brews, stores, and pours cleanly with minimal overhead. Commercial equipment enters the picture only when volume requirements genuinely exceed what a domestic-scale vessel can handle. A 2-quart pitcher requires 18 hours and two minutes of active time for batch preparation. Scaling that up to 64 quarts for an event is a different category of problem.

Be honest about your actual volume requirement before sizing up. For households, larger usually means wasted coffee and unnecessary fridge space, not better results.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a dedicated cold brew maker, or will any pitcher work?

Any airtight glass or stainless pitcher will hold cold brew during steeping and storage. A dedicated cold brew maker typically adds an integrated mesh filter basket, which simplifies the straining step. If you already own a fine mesh strainer and a leakproof glass pitcher, the functional difference is mostly convenience rather than cup quality. Start with what you have and invest in a dedicated vessel once you're sure the habit is sticking.
### How long does homemade cold brew keep in the fridge?

Cold brew stored in a sealed container keeps well for up to two weeks. Concentrate keeps slightly longer than ready-to-drink dilution because the lower water activity slows oxidation. Quality begins declining noticeably around day ten for most home brews , the flavor gets flatter and any fruity brightness in the bean fades. Airtight storage in glass, like the [Mason Jar Pitcher](product:mason-jar-pitcher-airtight-leakproof), extends that window compared to loosely sealed containers.
### What's the difference between the County Line Kitchen vessel and the Mason Jar Pitcher?

Both are 2-quart glass vessels with fridge-suitable lids. The [County Line Kitchen Cold Brew Maker](product:county-line-kitchen-glass-cold) is purpose-built for cold brew, typically including filtration hardware and a design optimized for the steeping and straining workflow. The [Mason Jar Pitcher](product:mason-jar-pitcher-airtight-leakproof) prioritizes airtight storage and a clean pour spout, requiring a separate straining step. Choose the former if you want an integrated process; choose the latter if you already have filtration handled and want better storage ergonomics.
### Is the SYBO commercial urn useful for cold brew at events?

The SYBO urn is a hot-brew percolator designed for volume service , it keeps beverages warm and brews fast, which are irrelevant features for cold brew. At the scale of a large catering event where hot coffee is the requirement, it serves a legitimate purpose. For cold brew specifically, no commercial urn design fits the steeping process, which requires still cold water and time rather than heat and circulation.
### How coarse should I grind beans for cold brew?

Cold brew works best with a very coarse grind , coarser than French press, roughly the texture of raw sugar or coarse sea salt. Finer grinds over-extract during the long steep time, producing a bitter, harsh concentrate. A burr grinder set to its coarsest setting is the reliable approach. Pre-ground supermarket coffee, even labeled for cold brew, is typically too fine and the grind consistency is poor , fresh beans ground correctly make a substantial difference to the finished cup.
